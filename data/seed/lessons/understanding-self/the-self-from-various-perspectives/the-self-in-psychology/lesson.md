# The Self in Psychology

Psychology gives us several testable lenses for asking how people think, feel, develop, regulate action, and describe who they are.

## Why this matters

Students often say, “That is just my personality,” when they are describing a habit, a temporary mood, a role, or a belief about their ability.
Psychology helps separate those possibilities instead of turning one observation into a permanent identity label.
It studies behavior and mental processes, so it can connect private experience with observable action and with the situations in which action occurs.
This makes the psychological perspective useful when you are planning a study routine, interpreting feedback, joining a group, or making a difficult choice.
William James reminds us that a person can be both the subject who experiences and the object who is described.
Humanistic approaches ask how the real self, ideal self, acceptance, and growth fit together.
Social-cognitive approaches show how beliefs about capability and feedback influence what a person attempts next.
Developmental theories show that self-understanding changes as people gain language, relationships, responsibilities, and new social positions.
Personality research adds patterns, but a trait score cannot tell the whole story of a person or predict every action in every setting.
The lesson therefore treats theories as maps: useful for asking better questions, incomplete as total portraits.
The goal is not to diagnose yourself or anyone else.
The goal is to explain one situation carefully, identify evidence, and keep your interpretation open to revision.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- **LO1 — Identify:** describe what a psychological perspective studies when it examines the self.
- **LO2 — Explain:** distinguish self-concept, self-esteem, self-efficacy, personality, identity, and self-regulation.
- **LO3 — Compare:** compare James's I/Me distinction with psychodynamic, humanistic, social-cognitive, and developmental lenses.
- **LO4 — Apply:** use one psychological lens to interpret a concrete student or group situation without overgeneralizing.
- **LO5 — Evaluate:** assess the limits of a self-related claim, score, or theory and state what additional evidence would be needed.

## Before you begin

There are no technical prerequisites for this lesson.
It helps to know that psychology is not the same as a personality quiz, a diagnosis, or a list of fixed labels.
The earlier lessons on philosophy, sociology, and anthropology provide useful comparison points because psychological explanations still operate inside relationships, institutions, and cultural worlds.
Activation question: when you say “I am good at this” or “I am not that kind of person,” what observation, memory, or feedback are you using?
One common misconception is that a theory proves what a person is; a theory instead organizes observations and makes claims that can be questioned.
Two-minute preparation: write one recent situation in which your behavior changed after feedback, a setback, or a change in audience.
Name the action, the thought or feeling you noticed, and the situation around it.
Do not use a private trauma, a health diagnosis, or another person's confidential story as your example.
Keep the example low-risk and specific enough that you can separate what happened from what you inferred.

## Vocabulary

### Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes.
Example: A researcher might study how sleep, expectations, and study conditions relate to recall.
Boundary: Psychology does not give every person a complete explanation from one observation.

### Self-concept

Self-concept is the set of thoughts, descriptions, evaluations, roles, skills, and characteristics a person uses to represent themselves.
Example: A learner may describe themselves as a careful writer, a sibling, a commuter, and someone still improving in mathematics.
Boundary: Self-concept is not a flawless inventory or an unchanging essence.

### Self-esteem

Self-esteem is the value or positivity a person associates with qualities and characteristics in their self-concept.
Example: Two students may both describe themselves as beginners, while one evaluates that status with patience and the other with harsh self-judgment.
Boundary: Self-esteem is not identical to competence, confidence in one task, or a permanent measure of worth.

### Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is a belief about one's capability to organize and carry out action in a particular situation.
Example: A student may have high self-efficacy for revising an essay but low self-efficacy for presenting it aloud.
Boundary: Self-efficacy is not the same as global self-esteem and does not guarantee success.

### Self-schema

A self-schema is an organized mental pattern about a self-relevant quality that helps guide attention and interpretation.
Example: Someone who sees themselves as a reliable group member may notice missed deadlines quickly and plan reminders.
Boundary: A schema filters information; it does not prove that every related event fits the pattern.

### Self-regulation

Self-regulation is the process of setting goals, monitoring progress, and adjusting action using internal and external feedback.
Example: A learner notices that late-night review is ineffective and changes the schedule before the next assessment.
Boundary: Self-regulation is not pure willpower detached from time, resources, emotion, or environment.

### Personality

Personality refers to relatively enduring patterns in how people think, feel, and behave.
Example: A person may often prefer preparation before speaking, while still speaking spontaneously in an urgent situation.
Boundary: A trait pattern is not a complete identity and does not remove the influence of context.

### Identity

Identity is a person's developing sense of who they are, including personally meaningful commitments and socially recognized positions.
Example: A learner may be forming an identity as a future nurse while negotiating family expectations and academic evidence.
Boundary: Identity is not a single label that outsiders can assign from appearance or one choice.

### I-self and Me-self

In William James's distinction, the I-self refers to the experiencing or knowing subject, while the Me-self refers to the self that can be noticed, described, and evaluated.
Example: “I am noticing my hesitation” combines an experiencing perspective with a description of a self-relevant state.
Boundary: James's distinction is a historical conceptual tool, not a claim that two separate persons live inside one body.

### Real self and ideal self

The real self is the person's current perception of who they are; the ideal self is the person they would like or believe they should become.
Example: A learner may currently avoid class discussion while hoping to become a more confident contributor.
Boundary: The ideal self is not a guaranteed future and the real self is not a final verdict.

### Congruence

Congruence is a close fit between a person's experience or real-self description and their ideal-self description.
Example: A student who values careful work and has routines that support careful work may experience a sense of fit.
Boundary: Congruence does not mean perfection or the absence of change.

### Reciprocal determinism

Reciprocal determinism describes how personal factors, behavior, and environment can influence one another.
Example: A supportive study group can make practice more likely, while the learner's preparation can also improve the group's climate.
Boundary: It does not mean that every outcome is equally under an individual's control.

### Locus of control

Locus of control refers to beliefs about how much outcomes are influenced by one's actions compared with luck, institutions, or other people.
Example: After a missed deadline, a learner may examine both their planning and the access problem that changed the available time.
Boundary: A belief about control is not proof that a person caused every outcome.

## Key ideas

### 1. Psychology examines the self through behavior and mental processes

Psychology asks how people perceive, remember, feel, decide, learn, and act.
The self appears in these processes as a subject of experience and as an object of thought.
A useful psychological question makes the process or behavior specific enough to examine.
Instead of asking “What am I really like?” ask “How do I describe myself before a presentation, and what changes after feedback?”
This shift does not make experience less meaningful; it makes the claim more inspectable.
It also allows competing explanations, such as preparation, anxiety, group norms, or prior success.
Example: A quiet student may be reflecting, conserving energy, uncertain about the topic, or responding to a discouraging classroom history.
LO1 is practiced when you name the behavior, mental process, and context without collapsing them into one label.
The next idea explains a historical model for the self as knower and known.

### 2. James's I-self and Me-self describe two directions of self-awareness

William James described a self that experiences and knows, and a self that can be experienced, described, and evaluated.
The I-self is not a hidden personality part; it names the first-person position from which experience is noticed.
The Me-self includes descriptions such as roles, possessions, relationships, abilities, and inner dispositions.
This distinction helps explain why a person can observe their own thought without becoming identical to that thought.
It also helps connect introspection with social and material aspects of life.
Example: “I am revising my outline” describes an act, while “I am a careful writer” is a Me-self description that may guide future action.
The description can be useful and still incomplete or mistaken.
LO3 is practiced when you compare the historical distinction with later ideas about self-concept and self-schema.
The next idea shows why a self-description is organized rather than a random pile of memories.

### 3. Self-concept organizes descriptions, roles, skills, and evaluations

Self-concept is the working representation a person uses when answering “Who am I?”
It may include physical characteristics, abilities, roles, relationships, values, memories, and aspirations.
Different parts can become more noticeable in different settings.
A student can be a sibling at home, a researcher in a laboratory, and a beginner in a new sport without becoming three unrelated people.
Self-concept also changes when new evidence is interpreted as relevant to the self.
Example: A learner who receives useful feedback may revise “I am bad at research” into “I need a clearer way to evaluate sources.”
That revision protects accuracy because it narrows the claim to a skill and a strategy.
LO2 is practiced when you distinguish a self-description from the value attached to it.
The next idea separates evaluation from description.

### 4. Self-esteem evaluates the self-concept, while self-efficacy predicts action in a task

Self-esteem concerns how positively or negatively a person evaluates qualities within their self-concept.
Self-efficacy concerns whether a person believes they can perform a particular action in a particular situation.
The two can move together, but they are not interchangeable.
Someone may value themselves as a person while doubting their ability to solve a new statistics problem.
Another person may feel capable in one task while judging themselves harshly overall.
Example: “I am still worthy while learning this” speaks to self-esteem; “I can complete the first three problems with a worked example” speaks to self-efficacy.
Specific goals and feedback can make self-efficacy claims more useful than a global statement such as “I am smart.”
LO2 and LO4 are practiced when you rewrite a global judgment into a task-specific, evidence-based claim.
The next idea explains how repeated self-relevant interpretations guide attention.

### 5. Self-schemas guide attention but can also narrow interpretation

A self-schema is a mental pattern that makes some information about the self easier to notice and organize.
It can support planning because people remember goals, responsibilities, and relevant evidence more readily.
It can also create confirmation pressure when a person notices evidence that fits and ignores evidence that complicates the story.
For example, a “not a leader” schema may cause a learner to overlook moments when they coordinated a group effectively.
The schema is not simply false; it may have developed from real experiences, but it may be too broad or outdated.
A careful learner asks when the pattern holds, when it does not, and what evidence would revise it.
This is different from forcing a positive statement that evidence does not support.
LO4 and LO5 are practiced when you test a self-schema against several situations instead of one memorable event.
The next idea introduces a theory that focuses on conflict and mental processes outside awareness.

### 6. Psychodynamic theories treat conflict and unconscious processes as relevant hypotheses

Freud's psychodynamic model proposed id, ego, and superego as interacting systems of personality.
In this model, the id represents demanding impulses, the superego represents internalized standards, and the ego manages demands in relation to reality.
These are theoretical systems, not physical organs located in separate brain compartments.
The model drew attention to motives and mental activity that may not be fully conscious.
Modern psychology does not treat every Freudian claim as equally supported or sufficient for explaining personality.
Example: A learner who wants immediate rest, values a deadline, and plans a realistic compromise can use the model as a way to map competing demands.
The map does not establish hidden motives or diagnose a disorder from one action.
LO3 and LO5 are practiced when you describe psychodynamic language as a lens and state what evidence it cannot provide.
The next idea turns from conflict toward the person's experience of growth and acceptance.

### 7. Rogers connects self-concept, real and ideal selves, and congruence

Carl Rogers's humanistic approach emphasizes the person's lived experience and capacity for growth.
The real self is the person's current perception of who they are, while the ideal self concerns who they want or feel they should become.
Congruence describes a closer fit between experience and self-description; incongruence describes a larger mismatch.
The mismatch is not automatically harmful because ideals can motivate learning and revision.
It becomes a useful question when an ideal is impossible, imposed, or disconnected from present evidence and resources.
Example: “I want to be a confident speaker” becomes more actionable when confidence is translated into a small behavior such as asking one question.
Rogers's idea of unconditional positive regard should not be reduced to praise for every action; it concerns accepting a person without making worth conditional on performance.
LO3 and LO4 are practiced when you compare an ideal, a current behavior, and a supportive next step.
The next idea adds motivation and a caution about hierarchical models.

### 8. Humanistic growth models can motivate reflection but are not rigid ladders

Maslow's humanistic theory is often presented as a hierarchy of needs culminating in self-actualization.
It can help learners ask whether physical safety, belonging, esteem, and opportunities for growth are affecting a goal.
It should not be used as a strict sequence that predicts every person's motivation.
People often pursue meaning, learning, care, or creative work while some needs remain uncertain.
Needs are also shaped by relationships, culture, institutions, and unequal access to resources.
Example: A student may be deeply committed to a community project while also trying to solve transportation or food insecurity.
The framework invites a broader question about conditions for growth; it does not rank people by how “actualized” they are.
LO3 and LO5 are practiced when you use the model to generate questions rather than a final judgment.
The next idea explains how learning and beliefs about capability connect the person with the environment.

### 9. Social-cognitive psychology treats person and environment as mutually influential

Bandura's social-cognitive theory emphasizes cognition, learning, behavior, and environment together.
Reciprocal determinism means that personal factors, actions, and surroundings can influence one another.
Self-efficacy is one important belief within this system, and it is usually specific to a task or setting.
People learn not only from direct rewards but also by observing models and interpreting consequences.
Example: A study group can model a useful revision method, while the learner's persistence can change the group's expectations.
This does not mean a learner controls the group, the instructor, or the available technology.
It means a psychological account can include both agency and constraints.
LO2 and LO4 are practiced when you identify the belief, behavior, model, feedback, and environment in a situation.
The next idea focuses on how goals become action over time.

### 10. Self-regulation turns self-knowledge into monitored action

Self-regulation involves setting a goal, choosing a strategy, monitoring evidence, and adjusting when the strategy is not working.
It is not a moral test of whether someone has enough willpower.
Attention, fatigue, emotion, time, feedback, tools, and social support can all affect regulation.
A useful plan makes the next behavior visible and gives the learner a way to notice progress.
Example: Instead of “I will stop procrastinating,” a learner schedules a 15-minute source check, records what was completed, and revises the next block.
The plan creates evidence about which conditions support action.
It also avoids treating one missed session as proof of a fixed character flaw.
LO2 and LO4 are practiced when you convert a self-judgment into a feedback loop.
The next idea examines development and the changing self across time.

### 11. Self-understanding develops through language, comparison, relationships, and life transitions

Developmental psychology studies how patterns change across the lifespan, including the growth of self-recognition and self-concept.
Children gradually use language, memory, social comparison, and group membership to describe themselves.
Erikson's psychosocial theory treats identity formation as a developmental challenge, especially during adolescence, but its stages are not a timetable that determines every life.
People may revisit identity questions after moving, changing courses, joining a profession, or losing a role.
Context matters because the feedback and opportunities available to one person may differ from another's.
Example: A first-year student may try out “researcher,” “caregiver,” and “leader” roles before deciding which commitments fit.
The process can include uncertainty without being a failure.
LO3 and LO4 are practiced when you describe identity as developing through evidence and commitments rather than as a single discovery.
The next idea shows how personality patterns fit into, but do not exhaust, the self.

### 12. Personality patterns are useful descriptions, not complete explanations

Personality refers to relatively enduring patterns in thought, feeling, and behavior.
Trait models describe tendencies that may be fairly consistent, while person-situation approaches ask when the tendency appears.
The same person can be highly organized in a laboratory and disorganized during a crisis without the observations being meaningless.
Assessment results also depend on the instrument, interpretation, context, and response style.
A score should not be treated as a diagnosis, destiny, or moral ranking.
Example: A learner who scores high on openness may enjoy exploring ideas, but the score cannot determine which career, culture, or relationship they will choose.
Psychological explanations become stronger when they connect patterns with specific behavior and acknowledge uncertainty.
LO2 and LO5 are practiced when you use a trait or test result as one piece of evidence among several.
The next idea brings the lenses together without erasing their differences.

### 13. A psychological explanation should remain plural, situated, and revisable

James can help us notice the self as subject and object.
Psychodynamic theory can help us ask about conflict and processes outside awareness.
Humanistic theory can help us examine meaning, acceptance, ideals, and growth.
Social-cognitive theory can help us connect beliefs, behavior, models, feedback, and environment.
Developmental and personality perspectives can help us track change and recurring patterns.
None of these lenses should erase culture, relationships, institutions, or material conditions.
Example: A student avoiding a presentation may be facing a self-efficacy belief, a previous social experience, an identity concern, or a practical language barrier at the same time.
A careful account names which lens is being used and what it leaves out.
LO5 is practiced when you state the claim, evidence, uncertainty, and next question.
This is the lesson's final method: explain the situation without shrinking the person.

## Worked example

### Example 1: “I am not a math person”

**Situation:** Mara receives a low score on a first statistics quiz and says, “I am not a math person.”

**Relevant information:** The quiz covered unfamiliar notation, Mara studied only by rereading, and she solved practice items correctly when she used a worked example.

**Reasoning process:** The statement is a broad self-concept claim based on limited evidence.
Self-efficacy may be low for this task, but the evidence does not establish a fixed ability or personality trait.
Self-regulation suggests examining the study strategy, schedule, feedback, and next practice conditions.

**Decision or interpretation:** A narrower account is more defensible: “I need a better strategy for translating the notation, and I have evidence that guided practice helps.”

**Possible limitation:** One quiz and one practice condition still do not show the full pattern.
The instructor's rubric, access to examples, and possible language demands may also matter.

**Learner self-check:** Which phrase describes the evidence more accurately: a global identity label or a task-specific hypothesis?

### Example 2: “I should always be confident”

**Situation:** Paolo wants to become a student leader and compares himself with a classmate who speaks quickly in meetings.

**Relevant information:** Paolo listens carefully, prepares agendas, and asks clarifying questions, but he hesitates when discussions move quickly.

**Reasoning process:** The ideal self is “confident leader,” while the current self-description highlights hesitation.
Rogers's lens invites attention to the gap, but it does not require Paolo to copy one communication style.
Social-cognitive theory asks which small behaviors, models, and feedback could build task-specific efficacy.

**Decision or interpretation:** Paolo chooses a concrete practice: open the next meeting with one prepared question and ask a co-facilitator for feedback.

**Possible limitation:** A single meeting cannot determine whether leadership fits Paolo or whether the group environment is supportive.
Culture, language, disability access, and power in the room may affect whose confidence is recognized.

**Learner self-check:** What evidence would show growth without requiring Paolo to become identical to the faster speaker?

## Common confusions

- **Confusion:** Self-concept and self-esteem are the same thing.
  **Correction:** Self-concept describes and organizes self-related information; self-esteem evaluates it.
  **Why it matters:** A learner can revise a skill description without treating their worth as a score.

- **Confusion:** Self-efficacy means believing that one is valuable.
  **Correction:** Self-efficacy concerns capability for a specific action; worth and global evaluation belong to different questions.
  **Why it matters:** Task-specific practice is more useful than a vague demand to “feel confident.”

- **Confusion:** The I-self and Me-self are two separate personalities.
  **Correction:** James used them to distinguish the experiencing subject from the self that can be known and described.
  **Why it matters:** The distinction clarifies self-awareness without inventing a split person.

- **Confusion:** Freud's id, ego, and superego are physical brain regions.
  **Correction:** They are theoretical systems in a historical psychodynamic model.
  **Why it matters:** A model can organize a question without proving a hidden motive or location.

- **Confusion:** Congruence means never changing the ideal self.
  **Correction:** Congruence concerns fit between current experience and self-description; ideals can be revised as evidence changes.
  **Why it matters:** Growth does not require pretending that the present is already perfect.

- **Confusion:** A personality test reveals the whole person.
  **Correction:** A test samples responses under particular instructions and requires careful interpretation.
  **Why it matters:** Scores should not become destiny, diagnosis, or a shortcut for judging classmates.

- **Confusion:** Self-regulation is only willpower.
  **Correction:** Regulation includes goals, monitoring, strategies, feedback, emotion, tools, time, and conditions.
  **Why it matters:** Changing the environment can be part of changing the behavior.

- **Confusion:** An internal locus of control means a person controls every outcome.
  **Correction:** It describes a belief about influence, not proof that institutions, luck, or other people are irrelevant.
  **Why it matters:** Agency and structural limits can be acknowledged together.

- **Confusion:** Erikson's stages predict exactly what every person must experience at a certain age.
  **Correction:** They are a developmental theory and a vocabulary for recurring questions, not a personal timetable.
  **Why it matters:** Identity can be revisited across cultures, roles, and life transitions.

- **Confusion:** Psychological explanations should ignore society and culture.
  **Correction:** Psychology can focus on individual processes while still examining relationships, context, learning histories, and environments.
  **Why it matters:** A person is not a mind detached from the worlds in which action occurs.

## Apply it

### Activity 1: Quick application

**Purpose:** Turn one global self-judgment into a specific, testable description.

**Estimated time:** 7 minutes.

**Materials:** Paper or a private note.

**Steps:**

1. Write one sentence that begins, “I am the kind of person who…”
2. Underline the broadest word in the sentence.
3. List two situations where the claim seems to fit and one where it does not.
4. Rewrite the sentence with a setting, behavior, or time condition.
5. Name one observation that could change your revised account.

**Completion criteria:** Your final statement is specific, includes evidence from more than one situation, and names a limit.

**Accessibility alternative:** Record the statement as audio or use a three-column sketch labeled claim, evidence, and limit.

**Privacy boundary:** Use an ordinary low-risk habit, not a confidential history or a mental-health label.

### Activity 2: Compare or analyze

**Purpose:** Compare how two psychological lenses explain the same behavior.

**Estimated time:** 12 minutes.

**Materials:** The situation below and a two-column note.

**Situation:** A learner stops volunteering answers after two classmates laugh at a mistake.

**Steps:**

1. Describe the observable behavior without calling the learner shy or weak.
2. Use a self-efficacy lens to ask what belief and feedback may affect the next attempt.
3. Use a humanistic lens to ask what self-concept, ideal, acceptance, or incongruence may be relevant.
4. Add one factor that both lenses could miss, such as language access, classroom norms, or fatigue.
5. Write one supportive next action and one piece of evidence you would seek.

**Completion criteria:** You distinguish the two lenses, avoid a diagnosis, and connect each claim to an observable detail.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use icons or a voice note for the two lenses, then state the limitation aloud.

**Privacy boundary:** Do not identify a real classmate or infer a private motive from public behavior.

### Activity 3: Transfer

**Purpose:** Use psychological ideas in a future study or work context.

**Estimated time:** 10 minutes.

**Materials:** A role you may take in the next year and a planning note.

**Steps:**

1. Choose a role such as intern, project member, peer tutor, or community volunteer.
2. Describe the ideal-self expectation you may bring to that role.
3. Identify one task-specific self-efficacy belief you need to test.
4. Design a feedback loop with a small action, a source of feedback, and a revision point.
5. State how culture, relationships, or institutional rules may shape the opportunity.

**Completion criteria:** The plan includes a concrete behavior, evidence, a revision point, and one contextual limit.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use a checklist, speech-to-text, or a diagram with arrows from belief to action to feedback.

**Privacy boundary:** Keep the future role hypothetical or use information you are authorized to share.

## Recall

1. What does psychology study when it examines behavior and mental processes?
2. How do the I-self and Me-self differ in James's historical account?
3. What kinds of information can be part of a self-concept?
4. How does self-esteem differ from self-efficacy?
5. What is a self-schema, and why can it both help and bias attention?
6. What do id, ego, and superego represent in Freud's historical model?
7. How do the real self and ideal self relate to congruence and incongruence?
8. Why is self-efficacy usually more useful when tied to a task and situation?
9. How does reciprocal determinism connect a person, behavior, and environment?
10. What evidence and limitation should accompany a psychological claim about someone's personality or identity?

## Reflection or transfer

Write about a study, work, or group situation in which your self-description changed after feedback.
Which part of the change concerns self-concept, which part concerns self-efficacy, and which part concerns the environment?

Consider a group or community expectation that influences the ideal self people are encouraged to perform.
What does the expectation make easier, and what might it make harder to notice or express?

Transfer scenario: You are supervising a new volunteer who says, “I am simply not good with people,” after one awkward conversation.
How would you respond in a way that respects the person, narrows the claim, identifies a next practice, and avoids promising a guaranteed outcome?
