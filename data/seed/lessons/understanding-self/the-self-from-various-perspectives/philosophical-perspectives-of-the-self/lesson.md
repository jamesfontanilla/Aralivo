# Philosophical Perspectives of the Self

Philosophy gives you several disciplined ways to ask what makes a person a self, rather than one final label to memorize.

## Why this matters

When a learner says “this is just who I am,” the sentence may hide a question about identity, agency, memory, body, or purpose.
Philosophical inquiry slows that sentence down so its assumptions can be examined.
This is useful when you are choosing a course, changing a habit, or deciding whether a past mistake defines you.
It is also useful in classroom discussion because two people can use the word self while answering different questions.
One person may be asking what is present in consciousness, while another is asking what remains continuous across time.
The difference is not a minor vocabulary issue; it changes what counts as a reason.
Studying several thinkers helps you notice that every account highlights some evidence and sets aside other evidence.
That habit protects you from treating a memorable slogan as a complete theory.
It also lets you compare an argument fairly before deciding whether it helps with your own question.
The goal is not to impersonate a historical philosopher or to force your identity into one school.
The goal is to build a provisional view that states its reasons, its limits, and the next question it needs to answer.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- LO1 — Identify the central philosophical questions used to investigate the self.
- LO2 — Explain the main claims associated with Socrates, Plato, Augustine, Aquinas, Descartes, Locke, Hume, Kant, Ryle, Churchland, and Merleau-Ponty.
- LO3 — Compare philosophical perspectives by their account of the self, evidence, continuity, and limits.
- LO4 — Analyze a claim about identity by separating its question, reason, assumption, and unresolved problem.
- LO5 — Create a short, provisional theory of the self that uses at least two perspectives and acknowledges a limitation.

## Before you begin

The previous lesson introduced the self as something that can be described through more than one lens.
You do not need prior training in philosophy, and you do not need to agree with any thinker in this lesson.
You only need the ability to distinguish an observation from an interpretation and a conclusion from a question.
Activation question: when you say that you are the same person you were several years ago, what makes you think so?
Possible answers include memory, body, character, relationships, commitments, consciousness, or a continuing life story.
A common misconception is that a philosopher’s view is a personal opinion with no reasons behind it.
Philosophical positions can be criticized, but criticism begins by representing the position accurately.
Two-minute preparation: write one current self-description and one reason you believe it.
Then write one challenge to that reason, such as a situation where the description did not fit.
Keep the example ordinary and private; you do not need to disclose trauma, health information, or family details.

## Vocabulary

**Philosophical inquiry** — a disciplined examination of concepts, reasons, assumptions, and implications.
Example: asking what would count as evidence that a person remains the same over time.
Boundary: inquiry is not simply collecting famous names or declaring a preference.

**Soul** — a term different philosophers use for the principle of life, thought, moral direction, or relation to the divine.
Example: Plato uses parts of the soul to explain conflict among reason, spirit, and appetite.
Boundary: soul does not have one fixed meaning across every philosopher or tradition.

**Consciousness** — awareness of experience, thought, feeling, or activity, considered from a first-person point of view.
Example: noticing that you are thinking about tomorrow while reading this sentence.
Boundary: consciousness is not automatically complete self-knowledge or a guarantee that every belief is true.

**Personal identity** — the question of what makes a person the same person across time or different experiences.
Example: asking whether a future version of you remains responsible for a promise made today.
Boundary: personal identity is not identical to a name, school record, or social-media profile.

**Substance** — in many historical theories, an entity understood as having its own kind of being rather than being only a temporary property.
Example: Descartes distinguishes thinking substance from extended bodily substance in his dualist framework.
Boundary: substance is a technical concept, not simply a synonym for physical material.

**Memory continuity** — a proposed connection between a present person and past experiences through remembered consciousness.
Example: remembering why you made a decision can make the earlier action feel like yours.
Boundary: memory can be incomplete, mistaken, or unavailable, so it cannot settle every identity question by itself.

**Bundle theory** — the view that what we call a self may be a connected collection or succession of perceptions rather than one separately experienced inner object.
Example: an afternoon may contain sensations, thoughts, moods, and images without one additional sensation called the self.
Boundary: bundle theory does not mean that experiences are random or that daily life has no practical continuity.

**Transcendental condition** — something required for experience or judgment to be organized, even if it is not an object found inside experience.
Example: Kant asks what unity must be present for different representations to belong to one consciousness.
Boundary: transcendental does not simply mean supernatural or outside all reasoning.

**Category mistake** — treating something as if it belonged to the wrong logical kind.
Example: asking where the university is after being shown every building may confuse an institution with a building.
Boundary: a category mistake is not just any false statement or disagreement.

**Embodiment** — the idea that human experience and agency are shaped by a lived body situated in a world.
Example: learning a dance involves posture, movement, timing, and space, not only an internal plan.
Boundary: embodiment does not reduce a person to a body viewed as an object.

**Provisional theory** — a reasoned account held for now and open to revision when better arguments or experiences appear.
Example: “My identity includes commitments that persist, but the way I live them depends on my body and situation.”
Boundary: provisional does not mean careless, indecisive, or impossible to evaluate.

## Key ideas

### 1. Philosophy turns “Who am I?” into several questions

The question “Who am I?” can ask about existence, knowledge, continuity, agency, value, or purpose.
Those questions overlap, but they are not identical.
A theory can be strong about consciousness and still say little about moral responsibility.
Another theory can explain practical identity without deciding whether a soul exists.
Philosophical inquiry therefore begins by naming the question before choosing an answer.
It also asks what kind of evidence could support or challenge that answer.
In a classroom, this move prevents a debate from becoming a contest between slogans.
For example, “I am my memories” answers a continuity question but may not answer what makes experience possible.
This supports LO1 because it identifies the problems that organize the historical perspectives.
Next, we begin with Socrates, whose contribution is a practice of examining how one lives.

### 2. Socrates makes self-knowledge an ethical practice

Socrates is known mainly through dialogues written by other authors, especially Plato, so scholars should be careful about assigning him a complete system.
The Socratic contribution emphasized in introductory lessons is the demand to examine one’s reasons, habits, and commitments.
Knowing yourself is not merely listing traits; it involves asking whether your beliefs are coherent and worth acting on.
This makes self-knowledge connected to ethical responsibility.
A person who notices a contradiction can revise a belief instead of protecting it only because it feels familiar.
The method uses questions, objections, and clarification rather than a private inventory of feelings.
For a student, the method can reveal the difference between wanting a goal and having a reason to value it.
It also reminds us that confidence is not the same as justification.
The limit is that questioning alone may not tell us every fact about memory, the brain, or the body.
This supports LO2 and LO4 by linking self-examination to reasons and limits.
Next, Plato offers a more structured account of inner conflict and order.

### 3. Plato explains inner conflict through an ordered soul

In the Republic, Plato presents a tripartite account of the embodied soul: reason, spirit, and appetite.
The model explains why a person can want something, resist it, and feel pulled by honor or anger at the same time.
Reason is associated with knowledge and guidance, spirit with assertive energy and concern for honor, and appetite with bodily and material desires.
Plato’s point is not that people are literally three independent persons inside one body.
The parts are a philosophical model for explaining patterns of conflict and order.
Justice in the person is connected with the parts performing their proper roles under wise direction.
This account treats a good self as something that can be educated and harmonized, not merely discovered as a hidden object.
It can help a learner analyze why a short-term reward competes with a long-term commitment.
The model’s limits include its historical assumptions about hierarchy and the fact that Plato’s dialogues do not all use one unchanging psychology.
This supports LO2 and LO3 by showing how a theory selects inner conflict as evidence.
Next, Augustine turns the examination inward and connects self-knowledge with memory, will, and God.

### 4. Augustine treats inwardness as a route to truth and responsibility

Augustine describes the mind as capable of turning inward toward memory, understanding, and willing.
His account is shaped by Christian theology and by engagement with Platonist ideas, so it is not a secular psychology in modern terms.
The inner life matters because a person is not understood only by observing outward actions.
Memory holds more than isolated facts; it can gather the materials through which a life becomes intelligible to the person who lives it.
Will matters because attention and desire shape what a person seeks and loves.
For Augustine, self-knowledge is connected to a higher source of truth rather than being self-sufficient mastery.
A learner can use this perspective to ask which commitments organize their memories and choices.
The theory also warns that a person may mistake possessions or passing pleasures for the deepest account of self.
Its limit is that its theological claims depend on commitments not shared by every philosophical framework.
This supports LO2 and LO4 by showing how a view of the self includes values and a theory of knowledge.
Next, Aquinas reframes soul and body as a unified human substance rather than two unrelated objects.

### 5. Aquinas describes the human being as a unified body-soul composite

Aquinas uses an Aristotelian framework in which the soul is the form or organizing principle of a living body.
This means the human person is not naturally a ghost operating a machine; the living human being is a unified substance.
The soul accounts for capacities such as life, perception, understanding, and willing.
At the same time, Aquinas gives human intellect a distinctive status, which creates difficult questions about its relation to bodily powers.
His view is therefore more precise than simply saying body and soul are the same thing or that they are completely separate.
It treats human actions as activities of a whole person with different powers.
For a learner, this can explain why a decision is both intellectually reasoned and bodily enacted.
It also offers a way to discuss human dignity without reducing the person to one measurable function.
The limits are that Aquinas’s framework is tied to medieval metaphysics and theological commitments.
This supports LO2 and LO3 by adding a unity-based alternative to stronger forms of mind-body separation.
Next, Descartes gives thought a central role in certainty and makes the mind-body problem explicit.

### 6. Descartes begins with the certainty of thinking

Descartes uses methodic doubt to test what can remain certain when sensory beliefs and assumptions are questioned.
The act of doubting itself shows that thinking is occurring, which establishes a thinker as a starting point for knowledge.
He then develops a distinction between mind as thinking and body as extended in space.
This is commonly called substance dualism, although the relation between mind and body becomes a serious problem within the view.
The point is not that bodies are unreal or that thoughts never depend on bodily conditions.
Rather, Descartes argues that the concepts of thinking and extension have different essential features.
The view makes first-person certainty important, but it may tempt readers to treat the mind as a private spectator detached from action.
It also raises the interaction question: how can a non-extended mind and an extended body affect each other?
A careful reader should separate the certainty of the cogito argument from every later claim in Cartesian metaphysics.
This supports LO2 and LO4 by distinguishing an argument’s starting point from its controversial consequences.
Next, Locke shifts attention from substance to consciousness and the continuity of a person.

### 7. Locke connects personal identity with continuing consciousness

Locke distinguishes the identity of a mass of matter, a living organism, and a person.
For personal identity, he gives a central role to continuity of consciousness across time.
Memory is important in explaining how past actions and experiences can belong to the present person.
This does not mean that a person is simply a perfect recording of every event.
Locke’s account creates questions about forgotten childhood, mistaken memories, and whether memory can prove the identity it is meant to connect.
Those questions became part of later debates, so the introductory version should not present Locke as ending the issue.
The strength of the view is that it links identity to responsibility and lived psychological continuity.
It also helps explain why a change in appearance does not by itself make someone a different person.
The limit is that consciousness and memory are difficult to define and may not supply a complete criterion in every case.
This supports LO2 and LO3 by changing the evidence for identity from substance to psychological continuity.
Next, Hume challenges the idea that introspection finds one simple self behind all experience.

### 8. Hume finds a changing sequence of perceptions rather than a simple self

Hume applies an empiricist test: if we claim to have an idea, what impression or experience gives rise to it?
When he looks inward, he encounters particular perceptions such as sensations, emotions, thoughts, and memories.
He does not find an additional, constant impression that is the simple self itself.
From this, Hume describes the self as a bundle or succession of related perceptions.
Association and causal links can connect experiences without adding a separate inner object to the collection.
The view explains why a person can feel continuous while also changing from moment to moment.
It does not require us to stop using names, promises, or practical identities in ordinary life.
Its challenge is directed at the metaphysical idea of an unchanging self that should be directly discoverable in experience.
Hume’s account also leaves a problem about how perceptions are related and how practical responsibility should be understood.
This supports LO2 and LO3 by providing a sharp contrast with substance-based and consciousness-based accounts.
Next, Kant argues that experience needs a unifying subject even if that subject is not an observable object.

### 9. Kant treats the self as a condition for unified experience

Kant agrees with Hume that the self cannot simply be found as another sensory item inside experience.
He argues, however, that different representations must be capable of belonging to one consciousness for experience to be intelligible.
This organizing unity is often discussed as transcendental apperception or the unity of consciousness.
Kant is not offering a photograph of an inner substance hidden behind experience.
He is identifying a structural requirement for thinking “I” across perceptions, judgments, and memories.
This lets him answer Hume without returning straightforwardly to a soul known as an object.
The view also limits what we can claim: the necessary unity of experience does not by itself reveal the self’s ultimate metaphysical nature.
For a student, the insight is useful when separate notes, observations, and memories must be combined into one reasoned judgment.
It also shows why a theory can explain the possibility of experience without describing a biography.
This supports LO2 and LO3 by separating an empirical self-description from a condition of coherent experience.
Next, Ryle questions whether the problem comes from treating the mind as the wrong kind of thing.

### 10. Ryle challenges the ghost-in-the-machine picture

Ryle argues that some mind-body problems arise from a category mistake rather than from discovering a mysterious object.
A category mistake places a concept in a logical category where it does not belong.
For example, a visitor who has seen every building may still misunderstand the university if they expect it to be one more building.
Applied to the mind, Ryle resists treating mental life as a separate inner ghost that controls a body from behind the scenes.
Mental concepts can often be understood through abilities, dispositions, reasons, and patterns of action in context.
This does not mean that feelings or thoughts are unreal or that people are only visible movements.
It means that explaining courage, attention, or skill may require describing how a person acts and responds, not locating a hidden object.
The view is a critique of a picture of mind, not a complete measurement theory of the human person.
This supports LO2 and LO4 by asking whether the grammar of a question has already created the problem.
Next, Churchland pushes the physicalist challenge further and makes its controversial status visible.

### 11. Churchland asks whether folk psychology is enough

Paul Churchland is associated with eliminative materialism, the view that some everyday mental categories may eventually be replaced by a mature neuroscience.
The target is not the learner’s experience but the explanatory framework used to describe it.
If a future theory explains behavior and cognition without concepts such as belief or desire, then some ordinary categories may not be fundamental.
This is a stronger claim than saying that the brain influences the mind.
It is also different from simply translating every mental word into a brain region.
The position is controversial because everyday psychological language remains useful for explaining reasons, plans, and social interaction.
It raises a valuable question about whether a familiar description is a scientific explanation or a practical shorthand.
For a student, the lesson is to distinguish a theory’s explanatory ambition from evidence that already exists.
Its limit is that neuroscience and personal meaning may answer different kinds of questions.
This supports LO2 and LO4 by showing how a physicalist account can challenge a theory without automatically settling identity.
Next, Merleau-Ponty returns the body to the center as the lived opening through which a person is in the world.

### 12. Merleau-Ponty presents the self as embodied and situated

Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenology begins with perception and the lived body rather than with a detached spectator.
The body is not merely an object that the self owns; it is also the medium through which the world is encountered and action becomes possible.
Skills can become bodily habits before a person can describe every movement in words.
A learner typing, cycling, or navigating a familiar campus responds through a practical bodily understanding.
This does not eliminate reflection, because a person can still examine and revise a habit.
It does challenge the idea that self-awareness is always a private, purely mental inspection.
The self is situated among places, tools, other people, language, and histories that shape what action can mean.
The perspective also treats perception as partial and revisable rather than as a perfect copy of the world.
Its limit is that embodiment does not by itself answer every question about persistence, moral responsibility, or ultimate purpose.
This supports LO3 and LO5 by providing a contemporary account that can be combined with, but not reduced to, earlier theories.
Next, we use a comparison method to build a theory without pretending that the thinkers agree.

### 13. Compare perspectives by question, evidence, and limit

A fair comparison starts by asking what problem each thinker is solving.
Socrates emphasizes examined living; Plato emphasizes inner order; Augustine emphasizes inwardness and relation to God.
Aquinas emphasizes the unity of human body and soul; Descartes emphasizes certainty in thinking and a mind-body distinction.
Locke emphasizes continuity of consciousness; Hume questions the simple self; Kant explains the unity required for experience.
Ryle analyzes the logic of mental concepts; Churchland questions whether everyday mental categories are fundamental.
Merleau-Ponty emphasizes the lived body and situated perception.
These are not interchangeable labels, and a list of names is not yet a comparison.
For each view, write the central question, the preferred evidence, the claim about continuity, and one limitation.
Then ask whether a second perspective adds information or changes the original question.
This supports all five outcomes because it turns historical knowledge into a repeatable reasoning practice.
The next section models the practice with situations that are new to the learner.

## Worked example

### Example 1: A forgotten promise

**Situation:** Mira promised to help a group member last month but now remembers only that a conversation happened.

**Relevant information:** A message confirms the date, Mira’s current intention is still to help, and the details of the original promise are uncertain.

**Reasoning process:** Locke’s perspective asks whether continuity of consciousness connects Mira now with the person who made the promise.
Hume’s perspective warns that present experience contains a shifting set of memories, feelings, and perceptions rather than a directly observed simple self.
Kant’s perspective asks what makes the remembered event belong to one continuing field of consciousness at all.

**Decision or interpretation:** Mira should not use uncertainty as proof that the earlier promise was made by a different self.
She should confirm the details, acknowledge what she can responsibly remember, and decide what commitment she can make now.

**Possible limitation:** These perspectives clarify the identity question but do not decide the social rule for repairing a missed promise.

**Learner self-check:** Which sentence describes evidence, and which sentence adds an interpretation about responsibility?

### Example 2: A body that changes a study habit

**Situation:** After moving to a new campus, Paolo can no longer study in the way that worked at home and concludes that he has lost his disciplined self.

**Relevant information:** The new space is noisier, the commute is longer, and Paolo still completes short tasks when the materials are prepared in advance.

**Reasoning process:** A Cartesian description might focus on Paolo’s thinking and intention to study.
Ryle might ask how discipline is shown through patterns of action and dispositions rather than as a hidden inner object.
Merleau-Ponty would ask how Paolo’s lived body, space, timing, and tools shape what studying feels like and makes possible.

**Decision or interpretation:** The narrowest current interpretation is that the old study routine no longer fits the new situation.
That interpretation is more useful than a permanent claim that Paolo has become undisciplined.

**Possible limitation:** A change in environment may not explain every difficulty; motivation, workload, health, and support could also matter.

**Learner self-check:** Which second piece of evidence would help distinguish a changed routine from a changed long-term commitment?

## Common confusions

- **Confusion:** Every philosopher in the lesson is answering exactly the same question.
  **Correction:** Some ask about certainty, some about persistence, some about moral life, and some about embodiment.
  **Why it matters:** Naming the question makes comparison fair.

- **Confusion:** Plato’s three parts of the soul are three literal people inside the body.
  **Correction:** The model explains kinds of psychological conflict and order.
  **Why it matters:** A model can be useful without being a physical diagram.

- **Confusion:** Augustine and Descartes both offer the same mind-body theory.
  **Correction:** Augustine’s inwardness is embedded in a theological account, while Descartes develops a modern argument about thinking substance and extension.
  **Why it matters:** Similar vocabulary does not erase different purposes and contexts.

- **Confusion:** Locke says memory alone is a perfect recording of identity.
  **Correction:** Locke’s account centers on continuity of consciousness, and it raises hard questions about memory gaps and mistaken recollection.
  **Why it matters:** A simplified memory slogan hides the problem the theory must address.

- **Confusion:** Hume proves that no person exists in ordinary life.
  **Correction:** Hume challenges the idea of an enduring simple self found as one impression; practical identity can still function.
  **Why it matters:** A metaphysical challenge is not automatically a denial of every everyday practice.

- **Confusion:** Kant discovers a hidden soul as an object.
  **Correction:** Kant explains a unifying condition for experience without claiming that the self can be observed like a thing.
  **Why it matters:** Transcendental reasoning and empirical observation answer different questions.

- **Confusion:** Ryle reduces people to visible behavior.
  **Correction:** His critique targets a category mistake and analyzes mental concepts through abilities, reasons, and dispositions.
  **Why it matters:** Critiquing a model of mind is not the same as denying inner life.

- **Confusion:** Embodiment means the self is only the body viewed as an object.
  **Correction:** Merleau-Ponty treats the lived body as the way a person is open to a world.
  **Why it matters:** It keeps action, perception, and context in the account of selfhood.

## Apply it

### Activity 1: Quick application

**Purpose:** Identify which philosophical question is hidden inside a self-claim.

**Estimated time:** 7 minutes.

**Materials:** A private note or paper.

1. Write an ordinary claim such as “I am the same person as before” or “I am not a math person.”
2. Label the claim as mainly about continuity, evidence, agency, purpose, or embodiment.
3. Name one thinker in this lesson whose perspective would make that question clearer.
4. Write one reason that perspective would notice and one limit it might leave open.
5. Revise the original claim into a narrower, provisional sentence.

**Completion criteria:** The response names one question, one perspective, one reason, one limitation, and one revised claim.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use a voice note or choose from the five question labels before speaking your explanation.

**Privacy boundary:** Use a low-stakes example and do not include another person’s name or private information.

### Activity 2: Compare or analyze

**Purpose:** Compare how different theories select evidence for identity.

**Estimated time:** 12 minutes.

**Materials:** A four-column table labeled question, evidence, claim, and limit.

1. Use the situation: “A student wakes after an accident with a changed body and incomplete memories but keeps a long-term commitment to care for a sibling.”
2. Fill one row for Locke, focusing on consciousness and memory.
3. Fill one row for Merleau-Ponty, focusing on embodied and situated agency.
4. Add a third row for a perspective of your choice.
5. Write a paragraph explaining why no single row answers every possible question about the person.

**Completion criteria:** Each row contains a distinct question, relevant evidence, a carefully worded claim, and a limitation.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use a comparison matrix with symbols, speech-to-text, or a partner who reads the situation aloud.

**Privacy boundary:** Keep the situation hypothetical and do not use real medical details about yourself or another person.

### Activity 3: Transfer

**Purpose:** Build a provisional theory of the self for a future decision.

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes.

**Materials:** A private note and the perspective comparison from Activity 2.

1. Choose a future decision such as selecting an internship, changing a study routine, or joining a group project.
2. State what your decision assumes about the kind of self you are becoming.
3. Use two perspectives to identify evidence that supports the assumption.
4. Write one challenge from a third perspective or from a different life context.
5. Draft a three-sentence theory of the self that includes continuity, change, and a limit.
6. End with one question you should revisit after gaining new experience.

**Completion criteria:** The theory uses two perspectives, names evidence, includes a challenge, states a limit, and ends with a revisable question.

**Accessibility alternative:** Record the three sentences as audio or use sentence starters supplied by a teacher.

**Privacy boundary:** Do not disclose financial, health, religious, or family information that is not needed for the reasoning task.

## Recall

- What makes a philosophical inquiry different from a personal label?
- Why should claims about Socrates be treated carefully when they come through dialogues?
- What three parts of the soul does Plato discuss in the Republic?
- How does Augustine connect inwardness with memory, will, and truth?
- How does Aquinas describe the relation between a human soul and a living body?
- What does the cogito argument establish for Descartes before later mind-body claims are considered?
- Why does Locke connect personal identity with continuity of consciousness?
- What problem does Hume raise when he looks for a simple self among perceptions?
- What does Kant mean by a unity required for experience, and why is it not simply an observed object?
- How do Ryle and Merleau-Ponty challenge an account of the self as a detached inner spectator?

## Reflection or transfer

Which perspective helps you ask a better question about a current study habit, and what evidence would that perspective fail to notice?

In a group or community situation, how might a person’s body, role, memory, or commitments change what others assume about their identity?

**Transfer scenario:** You are asked to recommend whether a classmate should lead a long project based on one confident presentation.
Explain how two philosophical perspectives would change the evidence you seek before making the recommendation.
State one limitation of your evidence and one question you would ask the classmate rather than infer from observation.
