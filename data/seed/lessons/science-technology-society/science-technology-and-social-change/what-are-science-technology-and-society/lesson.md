# What Are Science, Technology, and Society?

This lesson matters because the everyday systems around you are shaped by evidence, design choices, and social priorities at the same time.

## Why this matters

Science and technology are not distant topics reserved for laboratories or engineering offices.
They influence how students travel, communicate, study, work, and care for their health.
They also influence what a community can measure, build, regulate, afford, or imagine.
When a new tool appears, its effects depend on who designed it, who can access it, and how people use it.
An STS lens helps you ask those questions before treating a new system as automatically good or bad.
This matters in Philippine settings where decisions can affect islands, cities, farms, schools, households, and coastal communities differently.
It also matters in academic work because claims about technology often mix evidence with assumptions about people.
Learning to separate those parts makes your explanations clearer and your decisions more responsible.
The goal is not to distrust science or celebrate every invention.
The goal is to see relationships, limits, trade-offs, and possible next steps.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, you should be able to:

1. **LO1 - Identify** science, technology, society, and the STS approach using plain-language distinctions.
2. **LO2 - Explain** how scientific inquiry, engineering design, technology, and social context can influence one another.
3. **LO3 - Analyze** how values, institutions, resources, culture, and power shape a science or technology decision.
4. **LO4 - Apply** an STS lens to a Philippine or community example by naming evidence, affected groups, and limits.
5. **LO5 - Evaluate** a proposed action by weighing benefits, risks, access, environmental effects, and responsible participation.

These outcomes are observable: a strong response names a concept, shows the relationship, and gives a bounded example.

## Before you begin

There are no formal prerequisites for this lesson.
You only need one ordinary example of science or technology that you have encountered recently.
Activation question: who decided how that example would work, and who had a voice in the decision?
One common misconception is that science is only a collection of facts and technology is only a machine.
This lesson treats both as human activities with methods, institutions, designs, and consequences.
For two minutes, list one tool, service, or public system you used today.
Write what it helps you do, one kind of knowledge it depends on, and one group whose needs it may fit poorly.
Do not collect another person's private information for this activity.
Your notes can stay general: for example, “a campus Wi-Fi sign-in system” is enough.
Keep the example available because later activities will revisit it.

## Vocabulary

### Science

Science is organized inquiry that uses evidence, reasoning, measurement, models, and criticism to develop explanations about the natural or social world.
Example: a team compares water samples from different locations to investigate a possible source of contamination.
Science is not the same as a list of facts, a personal opinion, or a guarantee that every claim is final.

### Technology

Technology is a designed way of doing something, including tools, materials, procedures, infrastructures, and systems.
Example: a flood alert service combines sensors, software, communication networks, and a routine for sending warnings.
Technology is not limited to electronic gadgets and is not automatically the practical application of one scientific discovery.

### Society

Society is the organized pattern of relationships, institutions, practices, rules, identities, and shared resources through which people live together.
Example: a university community includes students, teachers, staff, policies, schedules, facilities, and informal norms.
Society is not a single person or a perfectly unified group with one interest.

### STS approach

The STS approach studies science and technology together with the social, cultural, political, economic, and environmental settings in which they develop.
Example: studying a vaccination program includes biology, logistics, trust, public communication, access, and policy.
The STS approach is not a claim that evidence does not matter or that every opinion has equal support.

### Scientific inquiry

Scientific inquiry is a process of asking answerable questions, gathering relevant evidence, and revising explanations when the evidence requires it.
Example: a researcher tests several explanations for why a crop disease appears after heavy rain.
Inquiry is not a single rigid recipe that looks identical in every field.

### Engineering design

Engineering design is a structured way to create or improve something under goals, constraints, and trade-offs.
Example: designing a low-cost water filter requires choices about materials, maintenance, safety, and local availability.
Design is not the same as proving a natural law, even though it can use scientific knowledge.

### Context

Context is the surrounding condition that changes what a claim, design, or result means in a particular place or time.
Example: a solar charging station may be useful in a remote area but less urgent where reliable charging already exists.
Context is not an excuse to ignore evidence or a way to make any claim true.

### Stakeholder

A stakeholder is a person or group that can affect, be affected by, or reasonably claim an interest in a decision.
Example: residents, commuters, drivers, workers, and emergency responders can all be stakeholders in a road redesign.
A stakeholder is not automatically right about every technical question, and influence is not the same as entitlement.

### Trade-off

A trade-off occurs when gaining one valued result requires giving up, delaying, or risking another valued result.
Example: a faster delivery system may increase convenience while also increasing packaging waste or traffic.
A trade-off is not the same as saying every choice has equal benefits and harms.

### Evidence

Evidence is information that supports, challenges, or helps refine a claim when its quality and relevance are considered.
Example: repair records and user reports can provide evidence about a public device's reliability.
Evidence is not any fact that happens to be available, and a single anecdote may not answer a broad question.

### Human flourishing

Human flourishing is a way of asking whether conditions help people live with health, agency, dignity, relationships, and meaningful opportunities.
Example: a digital service should be judged not only by speed but also by whether people can use it safely and fairly.
Flourishing is not a single score or a promise that one invention will make everyone better off.

## Key ideas

### 1. STS asks a relationship question

Science, technology, and society are distinct ideas, but they are not isolated boxes.
Science develops explanations and evidence through organized inquiry.
Technology creates designed ways to act, measure, communicate, make, or coordinate.
Society sets needs, rules, resources, meanings, and priorities that influence both.
In turn, scientific and technological changes can reshape work, relationships, institutions, and environments.
STS therefore asks both “what does this system do?” and “what conditions made it possible?”
This relationship question directly supports LO1 and LO2 because it requires accurate distinctions plus connection.
Next, we will make the relationship visible without collapsing the three terms into one.

![Science, technology, and society relationship map](/lessons/science-technology-society/sts-relationship-map.svg)

Use the arrows as prompts for questions, not as a claim that every relationship is equally strong in every case.

### 2. Science is more than a finished fact

Scientific knowledge includes observations, measurements, models, explanations, methods, communities, and criticism.
Researchers do not begin from nowhere; they choose questions, instruments, samples, concepts, and standards of evidence.
Results can become more reliable through repeated work, comparison, peer criticism, and public correction.
This does not mean that every claim is equally uncertain or that evidence is merely personal preference.
It means that a responsible claim should state what was observed, how it was studied, and what remains limited.
For example, a health study may suggest an association without proving that one factor caused another.
This supports LO1 and LO4 by making evidence and boundaries part of the definition of science.
The next idea asks what changes when knowledge becomes a designed intervention.

### 3. Technology includes systems and routines

Technology can be a device, but it can also be a process, standard, platform, building, network, or institutional routine.
A mobile payment service includes code, phones, accounts, identity checks, merchants, connectivity, and rules for resolving errors.
The visible interface is only one part of the system that makes the service work.
This broader meaning helps explain why a technology can fail even when the device itself functions.
Access, maintenance, training, cost, language, electricity, and trust may all matter.
Design choices also make some actions easier and others harder.
This supports LO1, LO2, and LO3 because it connects artifacts to institutions and lived conditions.
Next, we will examine how society shapes what gets designed in the first place.

### 4. Social context sets directions and constraints

Research and design do not happen in a social vacuum.
Funding, laws, markets, professional norms, public needs, cultural values, and available infrastructure shape priorities.
An institution may favor a project because it addresses a public problem, supports a business plan, or fits a policy goal.
Communities can also reject, adapt, or repurpose a design when it does not fit local practice.
These influences do not prove that research is fake or that technical performance is irrelevant.
They show why a technical possibility does not automatically become a public reality.
For example, a flood sensor is useful only when someone can install, maintain, interpret, and act on its signal.
This supports LO2 and LO3, then leads to the question of who receives benefits and burdens.

### 5. Science and technology can move together without being identical

Scientific inquiry and engineering design often support one another, but they answer different kinds of questions.
Science may ask how a system behaves or what pattern best explains an observation.
Engineering may ask how to create a workable solution under constraints of time, cost, safety, and materials.
Technology may emerge from scientific understanding, craft knowledge, trial and error, or combinations of these.
Technological tools can also expand what scientists can observe, calculate, test, or communicate.
The relationship is therefore reciprocal rather than a one-way pipeline from theory to product.
Keeping the terms distinct prevents the mistake that every invention is simply “applied science.”
This supports LO1 and LO2 and prepares us to study consequences rather than origin stories alone.

### 6. Effects are distributed across people and places

An innovation can create benefits for some people while imposing costs, risks, or new work on others.
The same platform may save time for one user, require unpaid digital labor from another, and exclude someone without stable connectivity.
Environmental effects can also appear far from the point of use, through materials, energy, waste, or land use.
An STS analysis names these differences instead of reporting only an average benefit.
It asks who is visible in the data, who is missing, and who gets to define success.
This does not require predicting every consequence with certainty.
It requires stating the evidence, considering affected groups, and marking uncertainty honestly.
This supports LO3, LO4, and LO5, leading to a closer look at values and power.

### 7. Values enter through goals and measures

Technical decisions always contain some idea of what counts as a good result.
Choosing speed over privacy, coverage over cost, or convenience over repairability expresses a priority.
Values can enter through the problem definition, the data selected, the design constraints, and the success measure.
Recognizing values does not make a decision irrational.
It makes the decision more open to discussion and revision.
For example, a campus attendance app may reduce manual work while creating concerns about surveillance and data retention.
A careful analysis asks which values conflict and whose voice is missing from the decision.
This supports LO3 and LO5 and leads to the role of participation in responsible STS decisions.

![A practical STS inquiry loop](/lessons/science-technology-society/sts-inquiry-loop.svg)

The loop is a checklist for inquiry; a real project may revisit earlier steps when new evidence appears.

### 8. STS is interdisciplinary and locally attentive

STS draws on natural sciences, engineering, history, philosophy, economics, sociology, communication, law, and environmental study.
No single discipline can answer every question about a complex system.
A technical performance question may need laboratory evidence, while an access question may need interviews or usage data.
A history question may reveal earlier choices that still constrain today's options.
Local knowledge can also identify conditions that a distant design team missed.
In the Philippines, geography, language, livelihood, disaster exposure, and public institutions can change what a solution requires.
This supports LO2, LO3, and LO4 by treating context as evidence rather than decoration.
The next section uses two original cases to practice the lens.

## Worked example

### Example 1: A campus water-refill station

**Situation:** A university plans to install filtered water-refill stations and asks whether the project is a good use of funds.

**Relevant information:** The plan includes filters, plumbing, a maintenance contract, student access, and a way to post test results.
The campus has several buildings, different class schedules, and students who carry different container sizes.
The proposal promises less single-use packaging, but the maintenance schedule and long-term water quality are not yet clear.

**Reasoning process:** First, identify the science question: what contaminants are present, how reliable is the test, and what conditions affect the result?
Next, identify design questions: where should stations go, how will filters be replaced, and how will faults be reported?
Then identify social questions: who can reach the stations, who pays for upkeep, and how will people trust the posted information?
Finally, identify environmental and equity questions: what waste is avoided, what waste is created, and who may still lack access?

**Decision or interpretation:** The project may be promising, but a responsible decision would fund a monitored pilot with clear maintenance, accessible locations, and public reporting.
The conclusion is not “the technology is good” in the abstract.
It is “this design is worth testing under stated conditions and with affected users involved.”

**Possible limitation:** A short pilot may not reveal long-term repair costs or changes in water quality during unusual weather.

**Learner self-check:** Did you separate the water-quality claim, the design plan, and the social conditions that determine whether the station works?

### Example 2: A community flood-alert group

**Situation:** A barangay considers using a group-messaging channel to share flood warnings during heavy rain.

**Relevant information:** The channel is fast and familiar to many residents, but not everyone has a smartphone, reliable data, or confidence in the message source.
Warnings may be based on official gauges, local observations, or forwarded posts, and those sources may not agree.

**Reasoning process:** Identify the evidence system first: what measurements or observations trigger a warning, and who verifies them?
Identify the technology system next: who can post, how messages are archived, and what happens if the network fails?
Identify the social system: which languages are used, who is responsible for people without access, and how residents can correct an error?
Compare possible harms: delaying a warning can increase danger, while an unclear warning can cause unnecessary travel or panic.

**Decision or interpretation:** Use the messaging channel as one part of a layered plan, with a named verification routine, plain-language messages, an offline backup, and a way to report updates.
The decision respects speed without treating a forwarded message as verified evidence.

**Possible limitation:** The plan may still fail during a power outage or when local observations are incomplete.

**Learner self-check:** Can you name one technical limitation and one social condition that affect the warning's usefulness?

## Common confusions

- **Confusion:** Science is only a pile of facts.
  **Correction:** Science also includes questions, evidence, methods, models, criticism, and revision.
  **Why it matters:** This keeps you from treating a claim as reliable without checking how it was produced.

- **Confusion:** Technology means electronic gadgets.
  **Correction:** Technology includes designed systems, procedures, infrastructures, standards, and tools.
  **Why it matters:** You can miss important causes when you inspect only the visible device.

- **Confusion:** Science creates technology in a one-way sequence.
  **Correction:** Science and technology can support each other, and technology can open new scientific possibilities.
  **Why it matters:** The reciprocal relationship explains why instruments and methods affect what can be known.

- **Confusion:** Society affects technology only after a product is finished.
  **Correction:** Social goals, funding, rules, markets, culture, and access shape design from the beginning.
  **Why it matters:** It shows where people can participate before a decision becomes difficult to change.

- **Confusion:** If a design works technically, it is automatically good for society.
  **Correction:** Technical performance is one part of a decision that may also involve safety, equity, environment, and trust.
  **Why it matters:** A working system can still distribute benefits and burdens unfairly.

- **Confusion:** Considering values means abandoning evidence.
  **Correction:** Values help define goals and trade-offs, while evidence helps test claims about likely results.
  **Why it matters:** Good decisions need both a defensible goal and a careful account of consequences.

- **Confusion:** A local or Indigenous knowledge practice is either identical to laboratory science or irrelevant.
  **Correction:** Knowledge traditions can use different methods, purposes, and validation practices; they should be described accurately rather than ranked by stereotype.
  **Why it matters:** Respectful comparison widens the evidence considered and avoids erasing community expertise.

## Apply it

### Activity 1: Quick application

**Purpose:** Practice separating science, technology, and society in one familiar system.

**Estimated time:** 8 minutes.

**Materials:** Paper or a private note, plus one ordinary tool or service.

1. Name the system without recording anyone's personal information.
2. Write one question the system depends on answering with evidence.
3. List the designed parts, procedures, or infrastructure that make it work.
4. List two groups that may benefit and one group that may face a barrier.
5. Write one uncertainty that a responsible decision-maker should investigate.

**Completion criteria:** Your note contains one evidence question, three technology elements, affected groups, and one uncertainty.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use a voice note, a simple four-box sketch, or dictate the response to an accessible writing tool.

**Privacy or safety boundary:** Use a public or personal-use example; do not name classmates, share accounts, or test a risky device.

### Activity 2: Compare or analyze

**Purpose:** Compare two ways of introducing a campus attendance system.

**Estimated time:** 12 minutes.

**Materials:** The two cases below and a four-column table labeled evidence, design, context, and trade-off.

1. Case A uses a QR code that students scan on their own phones.
2. Case B uses a staffed sign-in desk with a paper backup and an optional digital record.
3. For each case, name one strength and one limitation supported by the description.
4. Ask which students might be missed or burdened by each option.
5. Compare what each case assumes about connectivity, time, privacy, and staffing.
6. State one piece of additional evidence that would improve the decision.

**Completion criteria:** You identify at least two differences, one shared concern, and one evidence-gathering step.

**Accessibility alternative:** Compare the cases orally with a partner or use symbols instead of a written table.

**Privacy or safety boundary:** Discuss system features and access conditions only; do not collect real attendance records.

### Activity 3: Transfer

**Purpose:** Use the STS lens to frame a decision outside the examples in this lesson.

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes.

**Materials:** A local news item, public-service proposal, or campus decision that you can describe without copying its text.

1. State the decision in one neutral sentence.
2. Identify the scientific or evidentiary question and the design question.
3. Map at least three stakeholders and one missing voice.
4. Name one benefit, one possible burden, and one environmental effect.
5. Write a provisional recommendation with a condition for review.
6. Explain what new evidence could change your recommendation.

**Completion criteria:** Your recommendation links a claim to evidence, context, affected groups, and a revisable condition.

**Accessibility alternative:** Use a mind map, an audio response, or a small-group discussion with a shared summary.

**Privacy or safety boundary:** Choose a public issue and avoid diagnosing individuals, exposing private records, or proposing unsafe experiments.

## Recall

1. What does science include besides a collection of facts?
2. How is technology broader than a physical gadget?
3. What does society contribute to an STS analysis?
4. What question does the STS approach add to a technical description?
5. How can technology influence scientific inquiry?
6. How can funding, policy, or culture influence a technology's direction?
7. What is one difference between scientific inquiry and engineering design?
8. Why should an analysis name who benefits and who may face a burden?
9. Give one example of a value entering through a success measure.
10. What evidence or condition could make a provisional recommendation change?

## Reflection or transfer

Think about a tool or system used in your field of study. Which part of its operation depends on scientific evidence, which part depends on design, and which part depends on social rules or habits?

Choose a group or community situation in which a new technology is being considered. What would respectful participation look like for people who are affected but have less time, money, connectivity, or institutional power?

Transfer scenario: Your future workplace proposes an automated system that promises faster service. Before supporting it, write three questions: one about evidence of performance, one about affected people and access, and one about a condition that would trigger review.
