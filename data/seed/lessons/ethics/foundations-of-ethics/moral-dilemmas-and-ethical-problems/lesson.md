# Moral Dilemmas and Ethical Problems

This lesson helps you name what is in conflict, test whether the conflict is genuine, and give a responsible reason for what you choose to do.

## Why this matters

Ethical problems appear whenever an action can help, harm, respect, or disregard people.
They can occur in a classroom, laboratory, workplace, online group, family, or community.
The hardest cases are not solved by asking only what is convenient or what is allowed.
They require attention to duties, rights, consequences, character, relationships, and evidence.
Calling every hard choice a dilemma can make reasoning dramatic but imprecise.
Calling no situation a dilemma can hide the real costs of a decision.
A useful analysis separates an ordinary ethical problem from a conflict between serious moral requirements.
That separation helps you look for missing facts, overlooked alternatives, and people who may be affected.
It also helps you explain a decision without pretending that uncertainty has disappeared.
In study and professional life, a clear account of the conflict makes consultation and review easier.
The goal is not to produce a painless choice in every case.
The goal is to make the reasoning visible, proportionate, respectful, and open to correction.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. **LO1 - Identify** the agent, available actions, moral requirements, stakeholders, and constraints in an ethical problem.
2. **LO2 - Explain** the defining features of a moral dilemma and distinguish them from an ordinary hard choice.
3. **LO3 - Compare** individual, organizational, and structural levels of ethical conflict using evidence from a case.
4. **LO4 - Analyze** whether a conflict is genuine, false, avoidable, or intensified by missing information.
5. **LO5 - Justify** a proportionate response, including mitigation, consultation, documentation, and possible repair.

## Before you begin

You should already be familiar with moral standards, non-moral standards, duties, rights, fairness, and consequences.
If those terms feel new, review the preceding lesson before continuing.
There are no technical prerequisites and no need to disclose a personal experience.
Activation question: when two good reasons point in different directions, what would show that the conflict is moral rather than merely inconvenient?
A common misconception is that a dilemma means two choices are equally attractive.
In ethics, the more important question is whether two serious moral requirements apply and cannot both be fulfilled.
For two minutes, sketch a neutral case from school or community life without naming real people.
Write the decision, two possible actions, one affected person, and one fact you would still need to verify.
Keep the case hypothetical if the topic could expose someone to harm or unwanted attention.
You will use the sketch only to practice the analysis language in this lesson.

## Vocabulary

### Ethical problem

An ethical problem is a situation that calls for moral evaluation or a responsible choice.
Example: a group must decide whether to report a member's unsafe shortcut before a public demonstration.
It does not mean that every option is equally wrong or that no solution exists.

### Moral dilemma

A moral dilemma is a conflict in which an agent has serious moral requirements to perform two or more actions, can perform each separately, but cannot perform them all together.
Example: a student may have a strong duty to protect a vulnerable person and a serious promise of confidentiality.
It does not mean that any difficult decision automatically counts as a genuine dilemma.

### Moral requirement

A moral requirement is a reason with enough weight to make an action obligatory or strongly demanded.
Example: preventing a foreseeable serious injury can create a powerful requirement to intervene.
It does not mean that a personal preference becomes binding merely because it feels intense.

### Agent

The agent is the person, group, or institution responsible for deciding or acting in the case.
Example: a laboratory supervisor is the agent deciding whether to pause a procedure.
It does not mean that the agent controls every fact, outcome, or pressure surrounding the case.

### Stakeholder

A stakeholder is a person or group whose interests, rights, safety, opportunities, or relationships may be affected.
Example: students, staff, visitors, and future users may all be stakeholders in a campus safety decision.
It does not mean that every stakeholder has the same role, claim, or decision authority.

### Constraint

A constraint is a limit on what the agent can do, such as time, money, authority, information, or physical access.
Example: a deadline may prevent a committee from investigating every possible option immediately.
It does not mean that a constraint excuses avoidable harm or eliminates the duty to seek safer alternatives.

### Override

An override occurs when one moral requirement has sufficient priority in the circumstances to defeat or postpone another requirement.
Example: protecting someone from imminent serious harm may override a promise to keep a dangerous plan secret.
It does not mean that the less-preferred requirement has no value in every situation.

### False dilemma

A false dilemma is a conflict presented as having only two options when a third option, a priority rule, or more information could change the choice.
Example: “Either publish the private file or ignore the misconduct” may omit a protected reporting channel.
It does not mean that every apparent dilemma can be solved easily.

### Moral residue

Moral residue is the regret, grief, responsibility, or unease that can remain after a decision sacrifices a serious moral concern.
Example: a coordinator may still regret missing a deserving applicant after fairly allocating a scarce grant.
It does not prove that the decision was irrational or that guilt alone identifies the correct option.

### Ethical sensitivity

Ethical sensitivity is the ability to notice that a situation involves people, values, duties, or possible harm.
Example: recognizing that a data-formatting shortcut may affect a participant's privacy shows sensitivity.
It does not mean that noticing a concern already tells you what final action is best.

### Mitigation

Mitigation is an action that reduces the harm, unfairness, or rights-impact of a difficult decision.
Example: giving an affected student a safe appeal route can mitigate the effects of a deadline decision.
It does not mean that a small benefit cancels a serious wrong.

## Key ideas

### 1. Begin with the decision, not the label

First state what must be decided in one sentence.
Then identify who has the authority and responsibility to act.
List the available actions without assuming that only two exist.
Separate verified facts from predictions, interpretations, and rumors.
Ask who may be helped, harmed, excluded, exposed, or burdened by each option.
This first map prevents the word “dilemma” from doing the reasoning for you.
It directly supports LO1 because it identifies agents, actions, stakeholders, and constraints.
Once the decision is clear, the next step is to name the moral reasons attached to each action.

### 2. A moral dilemma involves more than difficulty

A difficult choice may involve money, time, skill, uncertainty, or competing preferences.
A moral dilemma adds a conflict between serious moral requirements.
The agent must be able to perform each required action on its own.
The agent must be unable to perform both or all of the required actions together.
The conflict should not disappear merely because one option is clearly permitted and the other is clearly required.
Many authors also reserve “genuine” for cases where neither requirement decisively overrides the other.
This is an analytical test rather than a promise that every philosopher accepts the same theory.
It supports LO2 by giving the learner a careful definition with boundaries.
After identifying these features, test whether one requirement actually has priority in context.

### 3. An ethical problem can have a clear best response

A problem can be ethically important even when the answer is not balanced between two duties.
For example, a group may have a clear duty to correct a fabricated attendance record.
Pressure, embarrassment, and financial cost can make the action hard without creating a dilemma.
The presence of risk or emotion does not by itself equal moral conflict.
An ethical problem may instead require evidence gathering, consultation, or courage.
It can also reveal that an earlier action was wrong and that repair is now needed.
The distinction prevents a learner from treating uncertainty as proof that all options are equal.
It supports LO2 and LO4 by separating difficulty from a genuine collision of requirements.
The following idea asks how missing alternatives often make a problem look narrower than it is.

### 4. Test for a false dilemma before choosing

Write the two stated options, then ask what a safe third option would look like.
Could the decision be delayed while urgent harm is prevented?
Could responsibilities be shared, sequenced, or transferred to a proper authority?
Could information be anonymized, corrected, or verified before action?
Could a policy be appealed, redesigned, or applied with an accommodation?
A third option does not automatically remove every moral cost.
It does show that the initial framing was incomplete.
This test supports LO4 because it makes alternatives and constraints visible.
Only after the option search should the agent decide whether a remaining conflict is genuinely unavoidable.

### 5. Moral reasons come from more than one lens

Consequences ask what each option is likely to cause and for whom.
Duties and rights ask what must not be done to people even when a benefit is promised.
Fairness asks how benefits, burdens, opportunities, and procedures are distributed.
Virtue asks what a responsible, honest, courageous, or compassionate agent would practice.
Care asks how relationships, dependence, vulnerability, and trust shape the response.
No single lens should be treated as a magic formula for every case.
Using several lenses can reveal a hidden stakeholder or a cost that a quick calculation misses.
This supports LO1, LO2, and LO5 by connecting standards to concrete reasons for action.
The next idea explains why a response can be responsible even when it is not cost-free.

### 6. A responsible response reduces avoidable harm

When a choice is necessary, first protect people from imminent and serious harm.
Then look for ways to preserve as many moral requirements as possible.
Use the least intrusive action that is adequate to the risk.
Consult a person with relevant authority without shifting responsibility blindly.
Document the facts, uncertainty, reasons, and safeguards that shaped the decision.
Tell affected people what they need to know, while respecting privacy and confidentiality.
Plan how to repair preventable harm and how to review the decision when new evidence arrives.
This supports LO5 because it joins judgment with mitigation, communication, and accountability.
The next idea widens the view from an individual choice to the setting that produces it.

### 7. Ethical conflict has individual, organizational, and structural levels

An individual dilemma concerns a person's direct choice and relationships.
An organizational dilemma concerns a team, school, workplace, or institution with roles and procedures.
A structural dilemma concerns connected policies, systems, incentives, and unequal access.
The same event can appear at more than one level at once.
For example, a student may face an individual disclosure choice inside a reporting system that is organizationally weak.
If the analysis blames only the individual, the system may remain unsafe for the next person.
If it blames only the system, the agent's immediate responsibility may be ignored.
This supports LO3 by showing how levels interact without treating them as rigid boxes.
The next idea applies the levels to power, authority, and organizational pressure.

### 8. Authority and policy do not settle every ethical question

A rule can help coordinate action, but following a rule is not identical to being ethical.
A policy may be unclear, inconsistent, unfairly applied, or unable to anticipate a new situation.
An institution may have more resources and power than the person who bears the burden.
That difference makes procedure, voice, accessibility, and protection from retaliation important.
The agent should ask who wrote the rule, who benefits, who is exposed, and whether an appeal exists.
Legal or institutional permission can be a relevant fact without ending moral evaluation.
Escalation should be proportionate and should use the safest available channel.
This supports LO3, LO4, and LO5 by linking ethical reasoning to governance and accountability.
The final idea addresses what remains after a difficult choice has been made.

### 9. Moral residue calls for learning and repair

A serious decision can leave regret even when the agent chose the best available option.
That remainder may signal a sacrificed value, an affected relationship, or a cost that deserves recognition.
It should not be used as a shortcut that labels every painful feeling as proof of wrongdoing.
After the decision, ask whether the process was fair, the facts were adequate, and the safeguards worked.
Invite feedback from affected people when doing so is safe and appropriate.
Repair may involve explanation, apology, restitution, policy change, or support for a person who carried a burden.
Reflection turns a single case into better preparation for future cases.
This supports LO5 because ethical responsibility includes what happens after the initial choice.
We can now use the full process in worked examples.

## Worked example

### Example 1: The private message and the unsafe demonstration

**Situation:** A student coordinator receives a private message saying that a group member plans to bypass a safety check before a public laboratory demonstration.

**Relevant information:** The message is second-hand, the demonstration is tomorrow, and the sender fears retaliation.
The coordinator can keep the message private, ask for verification, pause the demonstration, or report the risk through a protected safety channel.
The group member has a legitimate interest in fair treatment, while classmates and visitors have an interest in avoiding preventable harm.

**Reasoning process:** The agent is the coordinator, but the coordinator does not own the safety decision alone.
The moral requirements include protecting people from serious foreseeable harm and treating an accused person fairly.
The apparent conflict is not yet a genuine dilemma because a protected report and a temporary pause may preserve both requirements.
The coordinator should avoid public accusation, verify the technical risk with a qualified supervisor, and disclose only necessary information.

**Decision or interpretation:** Pause the risky demonstration long enough for a qualified person to check it and use the protected reporting channel.
Explain to the group that safety verification is required without naming the sender or announcing an unverified accusation.

**Possible limitation:** If the reporting channel is unsafe or unavailable, the coordinator may need a faster escalation route.
The decision could also be mistaken if the message misunderstood the procedure, so the technical check matters.

**Self-check:** Did you separate the safety requirement from the fairness requirement, and did you search for an option that protects both?

### Example 2: A limited student support fund

**Situation:** A student organization has enough money for either emergency transport for one member or accessibility improvements that would benefit future events.

**Relevant information:** The transport request concerns a same-day safety risk, the improvement has long-term value, and the organization has not yet asked its partner office for assistance.
The organization has a decision rule, but the rule allows an emergency exception and does not explain how to document it.

**Reasoning process:** The agent is the organization through its authorized officers.
The stakeholders include the member in immediate danger, future participants, donors, and volunteers.
The immediate safety concern has urgency, but the case is not automatically a genuine dilemma because an emergency grant, shared contribution, or partner referral may be available.
The officers should check the facts, protect the member's privacy, and identify whether a temporary transport solution can be combined with a staged improvement plan.

**Decision or interpretation:** Arrange safe transport first, record the emergency reason, and publish a transparent plan for the accessibility improvement.
Seek additional funds so the immediate response does not silently erase the longer-term duty.

**Possible limitation:** The organization may have no backup fund and may still need to choose how much to reserve for future participants.
That remaining trade-off is an ethical problem even if it is not a strict dilemma.

**Self-check:** Which facts changed the apparent conflict, and what would make the allocation process fair to people who are not present?

## Common confusions

- **Confusion:** Every hard choice is a moral dilemma.
  **Correction:** A dilemma requires competing serious moral requirements and an inability to fulfill them all.
  **Why it matters:** Precise labels reveal when evidence, courage, or a better process can solve the problem.

- **Confusion:** Two options are a dilemma because they are equally attractive.
  **Correction:** A moral dilemma concerns duties or moral reasons, not merely equal convenience or preference.
  **Why it matters:** Attractive options can still include one clearly overriding duty.

- **Confusion:** A policy decides what is ethical.
  **Correction:** A policy is evidence about procedure and authority, but it can be incomplete or unfairly applied.
  **Why it matters:** Ethical review remains necessary when a rule harms people or misses a safer option.

- **Confusion:** Confidentiality always defeats the duty to prevent harm.
  **Correction:** Confidentiality is important, but serious and foreseeable harm may justify limited disclosure through a safe channel.
  **Why it matters:** The relevant question is what disclosure is necessary, proportionate, and authorized.

- **Confusion:** A third option proves that the case was never serious.
  **Correction:** An alternative can reduce a conflict without making the original risk or moral concern imaginary.
  **Why it matters:** False-dilemma testing is a search for responsible possibilities, not a dismissal of pressure.

- **Confusion:** Moral residue proves the chosen action was wrong.
  **Correction:** Regret may show that a value was sacrificed; it must be assessed alongside reasons, facts, and process.
  **Why it matters:** Responsible agents can acknowledge costs without abandoning sound judgment.

- **Confusion:** Only a person can be responsible for an ethical problem.
  **Correction:** Institutions and systems can create incentives, procedures, and unequal burdens that shape individual choices.
  **Why it matters:** A complete response may require organizational or structural repair.

## Apply it

### Activity 1: Quick application

**Purpose:** Practice converting an emotionally loaded dilemma statement into an analyzable decision.

**Estimated time:** 7 minutes.

**Materials:** Paper or a private note; no personal case is required.

1. Write a hypothetical sentence beginning, “The person must choose between…”
2. Name the agent and replace vague verbs with two observable actions.
3. List one moral requirement connected to each action.
4. Add one stakeholder and one fact that needs verification.
5. Add a possible third action, delay, or consultation step.

**Completion criteria:** You have named the agent, two actions, two moral reasons, one stakeholder, one unknown fact, and one alternative.
**Accessibility alternative:** Dictate the six elements into an audio note or use a six-row table.
**Privacy and safety boundary:** Do not include names, confidential records, or details that could identify a real incident.

### Activity 2: Compare or analyze

**Purpose:** Distinguish a genuine moral dilemma from an ethical problem with a clear priority or missing option.

**Estimated time:** 12 minutes.

**Materials:** The two short cases below and a comparison table.

Case A: A student discovers that a shared file contains an unverified allegation and must decide whether to forward it before a deadline.
Case B: A student discovers a credible, immediate safety risk and must decide whether to use a protected reporting channel before a deadline.

1. For each case, identify the agent, actions, moral requirements, stakeholders, and constraints.
2. Mark which facts are verified and which are assumptions.
3. Test whether a third action or priority rule changes the conflict.
4. Classify each case as an ethical problem, a false dilemma, or a possible genuine dilemma.
5. Give one reason that could change your classification.

**Completion criteria:** Your comparison uses evidence from both cases and explains why the classifications differ.
**Accessibility alternative:** Complete one case in writing and record a spoken comparison for the other.
**Privacy and safety boundary:** Keep the allegation fictional and do not use real names or private messages.

### Activity 3: Transfer

**Purpose:** Apply the levels of ethical conflict to a future study or professional setting.

**Estimated time:** 10 minutes.

**Materials:** A blank three-column chart labeled individual, organizational, and structural.

1. Imagine an internship where a digital system excludes a user who needs an accessibility feature.
2. Describe the immediate choice faced by one worker or student.
3. Describe the workplace or school procedure that shapes the choice.
4. Describe the broader design, funding, or policy pattern that keeps the problem recurring.
5. Propose one immediate safeguard and one longer-term repair.

**Completion criteria:** Your response distinguishes all three levels and connects each level to a feasible action.
**Accessibility alternative:** Use icons, color labels with text, or a voice recording with three clearly named sections.
**Privacy and safety boundary:** Do not test an accessibility barrier on a real person or disclose a protected accommodation.

## Recall

1. What makes an ethical problem different from a merely technical problem?
2. State the core features that must be present before a case can be called a moral dilemma.
3. What is the difference between an agent and a stakeholder?
4. Why should verified facts be separated from predictions and rumors?
5. Give one reason a false dilemma can appear in a case.
6. How can an override change the classification of a moral conflict?
7. Compare an individual ethical conflict with an organizational ethical conflict.
8. Why can a policy be relevant without settling the whole moral question?
9. What is moral residue, and why is it not a complete verdict on a decision?
10. Name two actions that can make a difficult decision more responsible after it is made.

## Reflection or transfer

Think of a group project, organization, or community setting in which a rule and a person's needs might pull in different directions.
Which facts would you verify before calling the situation a dilemma?
Which stakeholder could be overlooked if the discussion focused only on the person making the decision?

Imagine that you are entering a profession where decisions affect people who have less authority than you.
What reporting, documentation, consultation, and repair habits could help you respond when a policy is incomplete?

Transfer scenario: A campus app is scheduled to launch even though testing shows that one user group cannot access a core function.
The launch team can delay the release, release with a warning, or ask for a limited launch while a repair is funded.
Explain how you would identify the moral requirements, test the available options, protect affected users, and review the decision afterward.
