# Communication Processes and Elements

This lesson matters because a clear message is built through choices, interpreted through context, and improved through feedback.

## Why this matters

Communication is part of almost every task a college student completes.
You ask a question, explain a proposal, write an email, join a group chat, or present evidence.
In each situation, the words are only one part of what another person receives.
The audience brings experience, expectations, language habits, attention, and emotion to the exchange.
A familiar phrase can sound helpful to one reader and vague or rude to another.
A message can also fail because of a weak channel, a distracting setting, or missing feedback.
Studying the process gives you a way to inspect what happened without blaming one person too quickly.
It helps you decide whether to revise the message, change the channel, check the context, or ask for clarification.
These ideas are useful in class discussions, research teams, student organizations, internships, and community work.
They also support respectful communication in a multilingual and culturally diverse Philippine setting.
The goal is not to make every exchange predictable or to remove all disagreement.
The goal is to make your choices visible, listen for evidence, and repair meaning when a message goes off course.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- **LO1 - Identify:** name the source or sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback, noise, and context in a communication event.
- **LO2 - Explain:** explain how encoding and decoding connect a person's intended meaning with another person's interpretation.
- **LO3 - Compare:** compare linear, interactional, and transactional views of communication and state one useful limit of each.
- **LO4 - Apply:** choose a message form and channel that fit a stated audience, purpose, relationship, and situation.
- **LO5 - Evaluate:** diagnose a communication breakdown using observable evidence and propose a respectful next step.

## Before you begin

There are no technical prerequisites for this lesson.
It helps to know the difference between a message and the meaning a person takes from it.
Activation question: Think of a recent message that was technically delivered but still caused confusion.
Possible misconception: communication is successful whenever the sender has sent something.
Sending shows that a message was produced; it does not prove that the receiver noticed, interpreted, or can act on it.
Two-minute preparation: choose one ordinary exchange, such as a class reminder or a group-chat request.
Write down who initiated it, what form it took, who received it, and what response followed.
Mark one detail you know directly and one detail you are only assuming.
Keep names, screenshots, and private details out of your notes.
You will revisit the example after learning how the elements connect.

## Vocabulary

### Communication process

The communication process is the changing sequence through which people create, convey, interpret, and respond to meaning.
Example: A student proposes a meeting time, the group reads the proposal, and members suggest a workable schedule.
Boundary: It is not a mechanical pipe that guarantees the same meaning at both ends.

### Source or sender

The source or sender is the person, group, or organization that initiates a message for a purpose.
Example: A student council becomes the sender when it publishes a notice about a campus activity.
Boundary: The sender does not fully control how an audience will interpret the message.

### Message

A message is the meaningful content expressed through words, images, sounds, gestures, timing, or a combination of forms.
Example: A short voice note can contain a request, a reason, and a tone of urgency.
Boundary: The message is not identical to the receiver's final interpretation of it.

### Channel or medium

A channel is the route or medium used to carry a message, such as speech, print, email, video, or gesture.
Example: A group may use a shared document for a detailed plan and a chat message for a quick reminder.
Boundary: A channel is not automatically appropriate just because it is fast or familiar.

### Encoding

Encoding is the process of turning an idea, feeling, or purpose into symbols and signs that can be conveyed.
Example: A team leader turns a broad concern into three written action points for a meeting.
Boundary: Encoding is not simply translating words; it includes choices about form, tone, emphasis, and timing.

### Decoding

Decoding is the process through which a receiver notices, interprets, and assigns meaning to a message.
Example: A reader decodes “Please review this today” as a request for comments before a deadline.
Boundary: Decoding is not a perfect recovery of the sender's private intention.

### Feedback

Feedback is a response that gives the sender information about reception, interpretation, agreement, or next action.
Example: A classmate replies, “I can meet at 4 p.m.; do you mean online or in person?”
Boundary: Silence is not reliable proof of agreement or understanding.

### Noise or interference

Noise is any factor that blocks, distracts, distorts, or competes with a message during communication.
Example: A weak connection interrupts a presentation, while unfamiliar jargon makes the content hard to interpret.
Boundary: Noise is not limited to sound; it can be physical, psychological, semantic, technological, or cultural.

### Context or environment

Context is the physical, social, cultural, relational, and time-based situation in which communication occurs.
Example: “We need to talk” may feel routine in a planning meeting but alarming in a private message from a supervisor.
Boundary: Context is not a fixed excuse that makes any interpretation equally reasonable.

### Audience

The audience is the person or group whose attention, knowledge, values, and likely response shape a message choice.
Example: A research finding may need technical detail for a panel and a plain-language summary for community partners.
Boundary: Audience analysis is not stereotyping every member of a group as identical.

### Purpose

Purpose is what a communicator hopes the message will accomplish, such as inform, request, explain, coordinate, or persuade.
Example: A reminder has a different purpose from an invitation to debate a policy.
Boundary: A stated purpose can be incomplete, mixed, or different from the effect a message produces.

## Key ideas

### 1. Communication is a process, not a single act

Communication unfolds through connected choices and responses rather than one isolated delivery.
A sender begins with a purpose, selects symbols, and sends a message through a channel.
A receiver attends to the message, interprets it, and may respond through words, action, or silence.
The sender and receiver can change roles as the exchange continues.
The process can succeed in one part and fail in another part.
Example: A notice may reach a student's inbox but remain unclear because the deadline is not stated.
This supports LO1 by making the elements visible in sequence and relationship.
It supports LO5 because a repair begins by locating the point of difficulty.
Next, we examine how an idea becomes a message through encoding.

### 2. Encoding turns purpose into a form another person can encounter

Before communicating, a person selects what to say, what to leave out, and how to organize it.
Those choices are shaped by purpose, audience, relationship, language, channel, and available time.
Encoding can involve words, images, emphasis, facial expression, layout, silence, or timing.
A sender may intend friendliness but encode it with a very brief phrase that looks abrupt on screen.
Example: “Send this ASAP” encodes urgency, but it does not explain what “ASAP” means for this group.
Good encoding makes the requested action, important background, and limits easier to see.
This supports LO2 and LO4 because learners connect message design with audience and purpose.
Encoding can improve clarity, but the receiver still has to construct meaning.
Next, we look at decoding and why interpretation is never just copying.

### 3. Decoding is interpretation shaped by experience and attention

Decoding involves noticing signs, connecting them with prior knowledge, and deciding what they mean.
The receiver may have a different vocabulary, role, expectation, or emotional state from the sender.
The receiver may also miss a detail because of fatigue, distraction, access needs, or competing tasks.
Two people can decode the same sentence differently without either person being careless.
Example: A message that says “That is interesting” may be read as encouragement, doubt, or polite closure.
Clarifying questions and paraphrases make the receiver's interpretation more visible.
This supports LO2 by separating intended meaning from received meaning.
It supports LO5 by treating interpretation as evidence to check rather than a character judgment.
Next, we add the message, channel, and environment that carry the exchange.

### 4. Message, channel, and environment work together

The message is the content, the channel is the route, and the environment is the situation around the exchange.
The same content can work differently as a spoken explanation, a poster, an email, or a live demonstration.
A channel can provide speed, recordability, reach, visual cues, or opportunities for immediate questions.
It can also remove cues, create access barriers, or encourage rushed responses.
Example: A complex laboratory procedure may need a shared guide and a short demonstration rather than one chat line.
Choosing a channel means considering what the audience needs to notice, remember, and do.
This supports LO1 and LO4 because it links an element to a practical design decision.
The environment also includes power relationships, privacy, noise, timing, and cultural expectations.
Next, we consider feedback as the part of the process that reveals whether a message is working.

### 5. Feedback turns a message into an adjustable exchange

Feedback can be a question, paraphrase, correction, facial expression, completed action, or request for more detail.
It gives the sender information, but it does not always give a complete or unbiased report of understanding.
A person may stay silent because they agree, feel unsafe, are busy, or do not know what to ask.
Useful feedback prompts a response without forcing the receiver to perform certainty.
Example: After explaining a task, a group leader asks each member to state their next step in their own words.
The leader can then correct a mismatch before the deadline rather than after the work is submitted.
This supports LO2 because feedback shows the process continuing through response and revision.
It supports LO5 because repair depends on evidence from the receiver, not just confidence from the sender.
Next, we examine the different kinds of interference that can interrupt the loop.

### 6. Noise can occur at any point in the process

Physical noise includes sound, poor lighting, distance, or an unstable connection.
Psychological noise includes worry, impatience, fear, or divided attention.
Semantic noise appears when words, symbols, jargon, accents, or examples do not carry the expected meaning.
Technological noise can alter timing, formatting, access, or the order in which messages are seen.
Social and cultural differences can create uncertainty about politeness, turn-taking, authority, or directness.
Example: A speaker uses an abbreviation that the team has never defined, so members plan different tasks.
Naming noise helps a group choose a repair, such as reducing jargon, changing the channel, or checking access.
This supports LO1 and LO5 by connecting a barrier to observable evidence and a possible intervention.
Next, we compare models that emphasize different parts of this complex process.

### 7. Models are lenses, not complete copies of real communication

A linear model shows a one-way path from source through message and channel to receiver.
It is useful for a public announcement when the main question is whether information was delivered.
An interactional model adds feedback and shows that people take turns as sender and receiver.
A transactional model treats communication as simultaneous, contextual, and shaped by relationships and shared experience.
No single model explains every event, and a simple model can still be useful for a narrow purpose.
Example: A campus alert can be analyzed linearly for reach and transactionally for how students interpret and act on it.
This supports LO3 because comparing models clarifies both their strengths and their limits.
The model should serve the question, not become a label that replaces observation.
Next, we apply the models to audience, culture, and purpose.

### 8. Audience, purpose, and culture shape responsible choices

Purposive communication begins with a reason for communicating and an audience whose needs must be considered.
Audience analysis includes relevant knowledge, language preferences, access needs, relationship, and likely concerns.
Cultural awareness means noticing that norms are learned and varied without reducing people to group stereotypes.
A respectful sender makes important assumptions explicit and leaves room for clarification.
Example: A student organization explains an event rule in plain language, offers an accessible format, and names a contact for questions.
The message can be concise without being incomplete or dismissive.
This supports LO4 and LO5 because effective choices balance clarity, inclusion, purpose, and evidence.
The next step is to diagnose a real exchange and choose a repair rather than simply rate it as good or bad.

## Worked example

### Worked example 1: The changing meeting time

**Situation:** A project coordinator posts, “Let us meet after lunch to finish the proposal,” in a group chat.

**Relevant information:** The group has members who attend different classes, “after lunch” has no shared clock time, and the channel is asynchronous.

**Reasoning process:** Identify the sender's purpose, inspect the message wording, and ask what the receiver must know to act.
The channel delivered the words, but the message did not encode a precise time, location, or response method.
Several receivers may decode “after lunch” through their own schedule and local habit.

**Decision or interpretation:** The breakdown is mainly an ambiguity in encoding and context, not proof that the group is uncooperative.
A repair would offer two exact time options, state the meeting channel, and ask members to reply by a clear deadline.

**Possible limitation:** The sender may know a practical constraint that is not visible in the chat, such as a room booking.
The group should confirm that the repair still fits access, travel, and class schedules.

**Learner self-check:** Which element would you inspect first, and what evidence would you want before assigning blame?

### Worked example 2: The feedback that sounded negative

**Situation:** A student receives the comment “You need to be more direct” on a draft community announcement.

**Relevant information:** The reviewer did not specify which sentence caused concern, and the student is worried about sounding disrespectful.

**Reasoning process:** Separate the feedback content from the student's first emotional interpretation.
Ask whether “direct” refers to the purpose, request, organization, tone, or amount of background.
The reviewer and writer may have different expectations about politeness and what the audience already knows.

**Decision or interpretation:** The best next step is a focused question: “Which sentence should state the action more clearly, and what tone should remain?”
The writer can produce a revised version and explain what changed.

**Possible limitation:** A single reviewer's preference is not the whole audience's response.
The writer can also test the draft with another relevant reader or compare it with the assignment criteria.

**Learner self-check:** How does the question turn vague feedback into usable evidence without dismissing the reviewer?

## Common confusions

- **Confusion:** Communication is complete once a message is sent.
  **Correction:** Delivery is one stage; reception, interpretation, and response still need attention.
  **Why it matters:** It prevents a sender from treating silence as proof of understanding.
- **Confusion:** Encoding is just choosing correct grammar.
  **Correction:** Encoding also includes form, emphasis, channel, tone, timing, and audience-aware choices.
  **Why it matters:** A grammatical message can still be unclear or poorly timed.
- **Confusion:** Decoding means finding the one meaning hidden inside a message.
  **Correction:** Decoding is an interpretation shaped by knowledge, experience, attention, and context.
  **Why it matters:** It encourages clarification instead of assuming that disagreement is bad faith.
- **Confusion:** Noise only means a loud sound.
  **Correction:** Noise includes semantic, psychological, technological, social, and cultural interference.
  **Why it matters:** You can choose a better repair only after naming the type of barrier.
- **Confusion:** Feedback must be a verbal reply.
  **Correction:** Feedback can be a question, action, gesture, correction, paraphrase, or carefully interpreted silence.
  **Why it matters:** The sender should make room for different ways of responding.
- **Confusion:** A transactional model says every person controls the exchange equally.
  **Correction:** Transactional communication is mutual and contextual, but power, access, and safety can be unequal.
  **Why it matters:** A model should not hide who can speak, decide, or refuse.
- **Confusion:** Audience analysis means predicting every individual perfectly.
  **Correction:** It means making reasonable, revisable choices based on relevant evidence and inviting correction.
  **Why it matters:** It supports inclusion without turning groups into stereotypes.

## Apply it

### Activity 1: Quick application

**Purpose:** Identify the elements in one ordinary communication event.
**Estimated time:** 7 minutes.
**Materials:** A blank note, a pen, or a private digital document.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a low-stakes exchange, such as a reminder about a class task.
2. Label the source, purpose, message, channel, receiver, feedback, context, and possible noise.
3. Mark each detail as observed, reported, or assumed.
4. Write one sentence explaining the most likely repair.
**Completion criteria:** You name at least seven elements, separate evidence from assumption, and propose a repair that fits the barrier.
**Accessibility alternative:** Use speech-to-text, a checklist, or a simple drawing of the process.
**Privacy or safety boundary:** Use a public or fictional example; do not copy a private message or identify a person.

### Activity 2: Compare or analyze

**Purpose:** Compare how two channels change the same message.
**Estimated time:** 12 minutes.
**Materials:** The message prompt: “Please send your section before Friday at 5 p.m.”
**Steps:**
1. Rewrite the prompt as a group-chat message and as a formal email.
2. For each version, state the audience, relationship, purpose, tone, and expected feedback.
3. Identify one possible noise source for each channel.
4. Explain which version offers better evidence that the receiver knows what to do.
5. Revise one version to add clarity without adding unnecessary length.
**Completion criteria:** Your comparison names at least two channel differences and links each revision to audience or purpose.
**Accessibility alternative:** Compare audio and text versions using a written transcript instead of recording your voice.
**Privacy or safety boundary:** Keep the sender and receiver fictional and do not send the draft to a real group without consent.

### Activity 3: Transfer

**Purpose:** Use the process to plan a respectful message in a new context.
**Estimated time:** 15 minutes.
**Materials:** A blank planning grid with purpose, audience, message, channel, feedback, and risks.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a context such as inviting neighbors to a clean-up or explaining a research result to a community partner.
2. State the action or understanding you hope to support.
3. Describe relevant audience knowledge, language, access, and relationship without stereotyping.
4. Draft a short message and choose a channel.
5. Add one feedback question and one repair plan if the audience interprets it differently.
**Completion criteria:** The plan makes purpose, audience, channel, likely noise, and feedback visible; it also names one limitation.
**Accessibility alternative:** Submit a storyboard, audio script, or visual sequence with alt text instead of a polished final message.
**Privacy or safety boundary:** Do not use a real person's health, finances, conflict, or contact information as the case.

## Recall

1. What is the difference between a message and a receiver's interpretation?
2. Name the main elements you would map in a communication event.
3. What does encoding do, and how can audience affect it?
4. What does decoding do, and why can two receivers differ?
5. Give one example of feedback that is not a spoken sentence.
6. Distinguish a channel from the message it carries.
7. Give one physical, one semantic, and one psychological example of noise.
8. What does a linear model show well, and what does it leave out?
9. How does a transactional model help you notice context or relationship?
10. What evidence would you collect before claiming that a communication breakdown was caused by the receiver alone?

## Reflection or transfer

1. In your studies, which communication choice most often affects your group work: timing, channel, wording, role, or feedback? Explain with one non-identifying example.
2. In a group or community situation, how could a feedback invitation make it safer or easier for someone to clarify a message?
3. Imagine you are starting a professional role where people use unfamiliar terms. What will you do when a message seems clear to others but not to you?
